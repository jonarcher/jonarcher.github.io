Contract Whist for Three Players

A plain-English guide to the rules, and to working out what to bid

The idea

Each round, you look at your cards and predict how many “tricks” you’ll win. You score points for every trick you win, plus a big bonus if your prediction was exactly right. Winning more than you predicted counts as a miss.

What you need

A standard 52-card deck. Aces are the highest card, then king, queen, jack, 10, down to 2. A suit is one of the four groups of cards: hearts (♥), diamonds (♦), clubs (♣) and spades (♠).

The words you’ll hear

Trick
One mini-round of play. Each player puts down one card, face up, and the highest card wins. The winner collects the three cards and keeps them in a pile in front of them. “Taking a trick” means winning one of these.
Lead
To play the first card of a trick. The suit of that card is the suit everyone else has to match if they can.
Follow suit
If the leader plays a club, you must play a club if you have one. You can only play something else if you have no clubs.
Trump
One suit is picked each round as the special suit. A trump card beats any card from any other suit. Even a 2 of the trump suit beats an ace of a different suit.
Deal
One full round of the game, from handing out the cards to counting the tricks. This game has 13 deals. The dealer is the person handing out the cards that round.
Stock
The cards left over after dealing. Despite the name, you never draw from it or play any of its cards. In other card games the stock is a pile you draw from, but here it is just the leftover cards. Its only job is to supply the one card that is turned over to decide the trump suit. The rest stay face down and are never looked at.
Bid
Your prediction, said out loud before play starts, of how many tricks you’ll take. Saying “three” means “I think I’ll win exactly three tricks.” You can also say “none”, meaning you think you’ll win no tricks at all.
Eldest hand
The player to the dealer’s left. They bid first and lead the first trick of the round.

The 13 deals

The number of cards each player gets changes every round. Deal 1 is 7 cards each. Each deal after that has one fewer card than the one before, until deal 7, where everyone gets just 1 card. Then it climbs again: 2 cards in deal 8, 3 in deal 9, and so on back up to 7 cards in deal 13. The game ends after deal 13.

The number of cards each player holds is also the number of tricks in that round. We’ll walk through deal 1, so everyone holds 7 cards and there are 7 tricks.

Walking through the first deal

1. Choosing the first dealer

Deal cards out one at a time. The first player to receive a jack becomes the first dealer. In our example, Player 3 is the dealer.

2. Dealing

Player 3 shuffles, then deals one card at a time, clockwise, starting with the player to their left (Player 1), until everyone has 7 cards. That uses 21 of the 52 cards. The remaining 31 cards form the stock: put them face down in the middle of the table.

3. Turning the trump card

The dealer takes the top card of the stock and turns it face up. Its suit is the trump suit for this round. Say it’s the 9♦. All diamonds are now trump cards.

This is the only time the stock is used. The other 30 cards in it stay face down and nobody ever sees them. Because the turned-up card is out of play for the round, nobody holds it, so it can’t beat anyone.

4. Bidding

Starting with Player 1 and going clockwise, everyone says how many of the 7 tricks they expect to win. Player 3, the dealer, bids last. (How to decide what to bid is covered in detail in Part 2.)

  • Player 1 looks at their hand and decides they will bid 3.
  • Player 2 has weaker cards. They bid 2.
  • The bids so far total 5.

The dealer’s restriction (the “hook”): the sum of the bids may not equal the size of the hand for the round. This is because the game is designed so at least one person must miss their bid. This means the dealer may not be able to bid what they wish to.

Here, 3 + 2 = 5, so the dealer, Player 3, can’t bid 2 (that would make 7). They can bid 0, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6 or 7. Player 3 bids 1, making the total 6.

You should write down each player’s bid so you can check them at the end.

5. Playing the tricks

Player 1 (to the dealer’s left) leads the first trick with any card from their hand. Going clockwise, each other player plays one card. The rules are:

  • If you have a card in the suit that was led, you must play one.
  • If you have none, you can play whatever you like. You can play a trump card to try to win the trick, or play a different card you want to get rid of (this is called “discarding”).

Who wins the trick:

  • If anyone played a trump, the highest trump wins.
  • If no trumps were played, the highest card in the suit that was led wins.

Example, trick 1 (diamonds are trump)

  • Player 1 leads the 8♣.
  • Player 2 has clubs and plays the J♣.
  • Player 3 has no clubs, so plays the 3♦, a trump.
  • Player 3 wins the trick, even though a 3 is a tiny number, because trump beats clubs.

Player 3 collects those three cards and leads the next trick. Whoever wins each trick leads the next.

Example, trick 2 (diamonds are trump)

  • Player 3 leads the 6♠.
  • Player 1 plays the Q♠.
  • Player 2 plays the 10♠.
  • Nobody played a trump, so the highest spade wins. Player 1 takes the trick and leads the next one.

Play continues until all 7 tricks are done and everyone’s hand is empty.

6. Scoring the round

Count how many tricks each player won. Every player gets 1 point per trick won, whether or not they were right. A player whose count exactly matches their bid also gets a 10-point bonus.

Say the final counts are:

  • Player 1 bid 3 and won 3. Score: 3 + 10 = 13 points.
  • Player 2 bid 2 and won 3. Score: 3 points, no bonus, because 3 is not 2.
  • Player 3 bid 1 and won 1. Score: 1 + 10 = 11 points.

The tricks add up to 7, as they must (3 + 3 + 1). A bid of “none” that works earns the 10-point bonus too, even though you won no tricks.

7. Next deal

The dealer’s job passes to the next player clockwise.

Every deal starts fresh from the full deck. Gather up all 52 cards: the cards each player won in tricks, the stock, and the turned-up trump card. Shuffle them all together and deal the new number of cards (6 each for deal 2). The player to the new dealer’s left bids and leads first.

Winning

After the 13th deal, add up each player’s points from all the rounds. The highest total wins.

Part 2: How to decide your bid

A note on sources. The Wikipedia article this guide is based on explains the rules but gives no bidding advice. The advice in this part is general trick-taking know-how, which we then tested by simulation: millions of computer-played deals of three-player contract whist, at every hand size from 1 to 7 cards. The figures below come from those runs, and the code and results are public. The simulated players all play their cards the same way, so real tables will differ. Even with good bids, expect to hit your number about half the time. It’s normal to miss often at first.

The goal: count your likely winners

Your bid is an estimate. Look at your hand and ask: which of these cards are likely to win a trick? Add them up and that’s your bid.

High cards matter, but not all high cards are equal. What matters is how likely a card is to be beaten. A trump is much harder to beat than a high card from another suit, and having many trump cards matters more than one lonely king.

What makes a card win tricks

These figures are for a player who tries to win every trick, in a seven-card deal.

  1. Trump cards are your best cards. Any trump beats any card from another suit, so the ace and king from the trump suit almost always win. Lower trumps win too: they win when someone leads a suit you don’t have and you play a trump card, and they win once the higher trumps are gone. Even a low trump (a 5 or below) won a trick more often than not.
  2. Aces in other suits are good, but not certain. Any suit that isn’t trump is called a side suit. An ace is the highest card in its suit, so no one can beat it with a card of the same suit. The only danger is a player who has no cards of that suit and plays a trump instead. A side-suit ace won about three times in four.
  3. Kings in side suits are a gamble. A king wins only if the ace of that suit isn’t played, or has already gone by. A side-suit king won about half the time.
  4. Queens in side suits win now and then. About two times in five.
  5. Low cards in side suits almost never win. A jack or lower won about one time in ten. A 7 of clubs will only take a trick if everyone else happens to have even lower clubs. Don’t count on it.

A useful fact about the turned-up trump card. It stays out of the game for the whole round, so nobody holds it. If the turned card is the ace of trump, then nobody has it, and the king of trump becomes the highest trump that can be played. If you hold the king in that situation, it is as good as an ace.

Adjusting the count

The table gives you a number like 2½ or 3¾. Here is how to turn it into a bid.

  • Round to the nearest whole number, and round halves down. 3¾ is a bid of 4. 3½ is a bid of 3. It is usually easier to steer away from a trick you don’t want, by playing a low card, than it is to find one you were counting on. In simulation, rounding halves down did slightly better than rounding them up.
  • Zero is a real bid. A hand with no trumps and no ace, king or queen is a good candidate for 0. A successful zero earns the same 10-point bonus as any other exact bid.
  • If the hook bars your bid, take the nearer number. The dealer can’t make the bids total the number of tricks. If your bid is barred, take the neighbouring number that is closer to your count. If your count is exactly the barred number, go lower. In simulation that beat going higher by about 1½ points a hand.

Small hands

Fewer cards change three things. A side-suit ace is worth less. Your seat starts to matter more. And a bid of 0 becomes the usual call.

The ace loses value. With larger hands you will get to lead at some point in a round, and you can lead with your ace then, while the others still have to follow suit. With smaller hands you lead less often or maybe never. Here is how often an ace of a non-trump suit won a trick, at each hand size, when every player tried to win every trick:

From 7 cards down to 3, the ace slips by about seven points per card. Seat hardly matters. At 2 cards the gap opens. That’s why an ace counts ¾ with four or more cards and ½ with three or fewer.

Your seat matters more. The eldest hand, the player to the dealer’s left, leads the first trick and so chooses the suit. With larger hands that edge is small. With hands of just two cards it is significant. With one it decides everything.

Zero becomes the usual bid. With three players, the average hand wins a third as many tricks as it holds cards: 2⅓ tricks at 7 cards, 1 at 3 cards, ⅓ at one card. In simulation the best bid was 0 for about 1 hand in 10 at 7 cards, 4 in 10 at 3 cards, and 6 in 10 at 2 cards or one.

The hook bites harder. In one-card deals the second bidder hit their bid 89% of the time and the dealer 43%. In 7-card deals the figures were 46% and 43%.

Here is what that means for the count:

  • In a three-card deal, use the counting table. Seat makes little difference.
  • In a two-card deal, count a side-suit ace, king or queen as in the counting table if you lead. If you don’t lead, count each of them at ¼.
  • In a one-card deal, the effect is at its largest. A side-suit card only wins if it is led, or happens to match the suit that was led. Here is how often a single card wins its trick, from two million simulated deals:
Your cardYou leadYou play 2nd or 3rd
Ace of trump suit100%100%
King of trump suit96%96%

The trump suit dominates. Even the 2 of the trump suit wins about 60% of the time, because with one card each, most opponents hold no trump at all.

Bid 1 if your card is a trump. Bid 1 with an ace, king or queen of another suit only if you lead. Otherwise bid 0. As dealer, the hook may decide for you. If nobody has bid 1, you can’t, so you bid 0. If one player has bid 1, you can’t bid 0, so you bid 1.

Six example hands

In the first four examples it’s deal 1 (7 cards each, 7 tricks) and diamonds are trump, because the 9♦ was turned over from the stock. That means nobody holds the 9♦. Diamonds are trump in the last two as well. In all six you bid first. The “simulation” line says how often each bid came out exactly right when we played the hand against random opponents.

Hand 1: strong trumps and an ace

A♦ K♦ 7♦ A♠ 9♣ 4♣ J♥
3¾count

A♦ K♦ 7♦ = 3 (every trump is 1), A♠ = ¾. Everything else = 0.

4bid

Decision. 3¾ rounds to 4. In simulation, a bid of 4 came out exactly right 45% of the time and a bid of 5 came out right 36% of the time.

Hand 2: a hand built for zero

9♠ 6♠ 2♠ 8♥ 3♥ 7♣ 5♣
0count

No trumps, no aces, kings or queens.

0bid

Decision. Bid 0. In every suit you hold, you have a low card to follow with, and if diamonds are led you can discard anything you like. Your highest card is only a 9. The risk with a zero bid is being forced to follow suit with a high card, and this hand barely has any. In simulation, a bid of 0 came out exactly right 79% of the time. A bid of 1 came out right 37% of the time.

Hand 3: lots of trumps, no ace or king of trump

Q♦ J♦ 10♦ 8♦ 3♦ K♠ 4♥
5½count

Five trumps = 5. K♠ = ½.

5bid

Decision. 5½ is exactly halfway, so round down to 5. There are 13 diamonds. One is the face-up 9♦ and you hold five, so 7 are hidden somewhere. The other two players hold only 14 cards between them, out of the 44 cards you can’t see, so on average they have only about two of those seven trumps. You hold most of the trumps in play. In simulation, a bid of 5 came out exactly right 45% of the time, a bid of 6 came out right 27% of the time, and a bid of 4 came out right 23% of the time.

Hand 4: two kings and two small trumps

K♥ 5♥ K♠ Q♣ 3♣ 6♦ 2♦
3¼count

3bid

Decision. 3¼ rounds to 3. A bid of 2 is nearly as good. In simulation, a bid of 2 came out exactly right 44% of the time and a bid of 3 came out right 41% of the time. This is the kind of hand where the count can’t settle it.

Decision. 1½ is exactly halfway, so round down to 1. In simulation, a bid of 1 came out exactly right 47% of the time and a bid of 2 came out right 40% of the time. The choice is close.

1½count

Decision. Round 1½ down to 1. In simulation, a bid of 1 came out exactly right 44% of the time, a bid of 2 came out right 39% of the time, and a bid of 0 came out right 14% of the time. If you didn’t lead, the ace would count only ¼.

Using what the other players have bid

You bid after the players to your right, so their bids give you clues. Compare the total so far to the number of tricks available. If the bids add up to well under, there are tricks that nobody asked for, and they have to land on someone. If they add up to well over, some players will be disappointed.

In simulation, this clue was worth using only as dealer, in deals of four or more cards. Dealers did best when they leaned a little toward the number of tricks nobody had bid for. One earlier bid, as the second player has, told too little to help.

Example: deal 1 has 7 tricks. Player 1 bids 1 and Player 2 bids 1. That’s only 2 of the 7, so 5 tricks have nobody’s name on them. If you’re Player 3 (the dealer) you cannot bid 5, because that would make the total exactly 7. Bidding 3 or 4 is quite possible with a decent hand, because the other two players have told you their hands are weak.

These are clues, not rules. Players sometimes bid low on purpose or misjudge their hands.

Playing to hit your bid

A bid isn’t just a prediction you sit and wait on. During play you steer toward your number, and this is a big part of the fun. Remember that taking too many tricks is as bad as taking too few.

If you still need tricks: lead your strong cards early, such as aces and high trumps, before someone else can trump them or the ace gets played.

If you’ve already won your bid: now you want to lose every remaining trick. Follow suit with your lowest card of that suit, and when you can’t follow suit, discard your most dangerous high card instead of trumping.

Example: you bid 2 and have already won 2 tricks, with 3 tricks left. Your remaining cards are K♣, 5♣ and 8♥ (diamonds are trump). Someone leads a club: you must follow suit, so play the 5♣, not the king. Later someone leads a diamond and you have none: you can discard, so get rid of the K♣, the card most likely to win a trick by accident.

Cheat sheet: bidding in six steps

  1. Count every trump as 1.
  2. Add ¾ for each ace in another suit (½ in deals of 3 cards or fewer), ½ for each king and ¼ for each queen.
  3. In deals of one or two cards, seat matters. With one card, bid 1 for a trump, or for an ace, king or queen if you lead. Otherwise bid 0. With two cards, count side-suit aces, kings and queens at ¼ each if you don’t lead.
  4. Round to the nearest whole number, halves down. If the hook bars your bid, take the neighbouring number nearer your count, and go lower if it’s a tie.
  5. As dealer in deals of four or more cards, lean a little toward the number of tricks nobody has bid for.
  6. Then steer. Once the tricks start, play to hit your number exactly: no more, no fewer.
